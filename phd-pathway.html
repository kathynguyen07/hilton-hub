1BS/MS Plan (4+1)▸
1Definition & Purpose
▸

4+1 is an accelerated pathway, not a separate degree — it allows BS Global Hospitality Leadership (GHL) students to begin taking graduate-level courses from the MS in Hospitality Management (also known as "Strategic Hospitality Leadership") while still an undergraduate, shortening the total time to earn both degrees from six years to five.

Official purpose: to retain strong GHL students at Hilton College for graduate study, rather than seeing them graduate with a BS and leave for elsewhere or enter the workforce directly.

2Outcome
▸

Since 4+1 is only a pathway, its actual outcome is the outcome of the MS in Hospitality Management it leads to (see Filter 2) — 4+1 itself does not produce a separate degree.

The specific benefit of 4+1: earning both the BS and MS at a younger age (~22–23 instead of ~23–24), entering the job market a year earlier with an MS in hand, or transitioning into a PhD a year sooner.

4Application Process — Step by Step
▸
  • Meet with the undergraduate academic advisor to express intent to join 4+1 — ideally as soon as Junior standing is close.
  • The advisor confirms eligibility (60–89 credits, GPA ≥2.00) → refers the student to the Graduate Programs Office.
  • The Graduate Program Manager invites the student for an interview.
  • If the interview is successful → admission into 4+1 begins the following registration cycle.
  • Each subsequent term: continue meeting with the undergraduate academic advisor to determine which courses count toward both the BS and the MS.
  • Once each term's course schedule is published: coordinate with the Graduate Program Manager to confirm which 4+1 courses are approved.
  • Important registration detail: 4+1 students cannot use myUH to self-register for graduate-level courses while holding undergraduate status — registration must go manually through the Graduate Program Manager.
  • At BS graduation: submit a formal application for admission to the MS (same process as any new MS applicant).

2MS in Hospitality Management▸
1Definition & Purpose
▸

Official description: "designed to meet the hospitality industry's need for individuals who have a variety of professional and academic skills and advanced knowledge." This is an advanced master's degree for those who want to deepen management expertise (not relearn basic operations as in the BS), or pivot into hospitality from a different background.

Key distinction from the Executive MHM (a separate online program): the official FAQ states — "the traditional master's program is designed for students who have limited professional and managerial experience" — meaning the standard MS (including via 4+1) targets those with limited management experience, while the Executive MHM is built for those with many years of management experience.

2Outcome — Comparing the Two Tracks
▸

The official page describes one shared outcome for the whole program (not split by track): graduates can take on roles such as hotel manager, event coordinator, or food and beverage director; third-party analysis adds hotel operations, revenue management, human resources, and hospitality finance. But this outcome reflects the Professional Track. The two tracks actually lead to very different outcomes:

Professional TrackResearch Track
Direct career outcomeOperating management roles: hotel manager, F&B director, event coordinator, revenue managerNot aimed at one specific operating role — the outcome is research capability itself, not a management title
Capstone productProfessional Paper — solves a real operating problem at the student's workplace (via HRMA 6360 Practicum)A complete Thesis — literature review, methodology, data collection, analysis, results, written to academic standard
Core skill builtOperations, HR/financial/marketing management at a strategic levelResearch design (HRMA 6190), literature evaluation/analysis (HRMA 6382), statistical analysis (HRMA 6330) — the skillset of a researcher, not a manager
If pursuing a PhD nextNot designed for this purpose — missing both foundational methodology coursesDesigned specifically for this purpose — the official page states: "designed for students who wish to conduct research and contribute new knowledge to a specific field and are interested in pursuing a Ph.D. after completing their MS degree"

Why the Research Track is the "open door" to a PhD:

  • It meets the PhD's degree requirement directly — the PhD asks for "MS in Hospitality Management or equivalent, GPA ≥3.2/4.0," and the Research Track is built explicitly as that stepping stone.
  • The thesis is the first independent research portfolio — a complete body of work from research question to methodology to data collection to results — stronger evidence of "prior research experience" in a PhD application than a professional paper alone.
  • Two methodology courses map directly onto the PhD candidacy exam: HRMA 6190 (Research Proposal) and HRMA 6382 (Methods of Research) are effectively a smaller-scale rehearsal of the same competencies tested at candidacy; HRMA 6330 (Statistical Data Analysis) is the foundation for the PhD's Multivariate Analysis course.
  • Colloquium (HRMA 6101) builds the habit of ongoing academic critique — a skill needed throughout the PhD (dissertation defense, seminars).
  • The relationship with a faculty advisor can extend from MS into PhD. The thesis requires direct guidance from one advisor throughout — an opportunity to build a relationship strong enough for that person (or a departmental colleague) to become a strong letter-writer, or even a future PhD advisor.

Limitation to note: the Research Track's capstone does NOT automatically lead to a publication — the thesis is an internal document filed through UH Libraries' Electronic Thesis and Dissertation (ETD) system, not the same as a journal publication. Turning it into an actual publication requires proactively discussing that possibility with the advisor after completion; it isn't a built-in program feature.

3PhD in Hospitality Administration▸
1Definition & Purpose
▸

Official description: a program "designed to provide flexibility, independence, and interdisciplinary study to support students' individual research interests," preparing students for careers in academia and for deep research roles within the hospitality industry. This is a three-year, in-residence, full-time program — not online, not part-time.

Fundamental difference from the MS (even the Research Track): the MS teaches "how to do research" at a foundational level; the PhD trains a student to independently create new research, teach, and secure research funding — producing a complete scholar/professor, not just a strong analyst.

2Outcome
▸

The official description develops two groups of competencies:

Teaching & Instructional Development:

  • Develop teaching skills at both undergraduate and graduate level
  • Communicate research results effectively to both academia and industry
  • Build grant-proposal writing skills and develop research funding sources

Research & Knowledge Development:

  • Contribute to the hospitality field through research and knowledge-sharing
  • Develop theory and expand knowledge in the field
  • Identify and analyze problems within the hospitality context
  • Evaluate and develop solutions using both quantitative and qualitative methods

Key difference from the MS: the official page states that "all students further develop and publish research in collaboration with faculty during the program" — publication is a default expectation throughout the PhD, not optional as it is at the MS Research Track stage (where the thesis is only an internal document and publication must be self-initiated).

Real-world evidence: several current and former Hilton College PhD students have co-published with faculty during the program — for example, a PhD candidate publishing with two faculty members on "On-Farm Produce Safety Practices Assessment in Texas" in Food Protection Trends; and former PhD graduates publishing with faculty in the Journal of Food Protection.

4Research & Mentorship▸
PURSProvost's Undergraduate Research Scholarship
▸

FULL DEFINITION

PURS is a program managed by the Office of Undergraduate Research and Major Awards (OURMA) under the Honors College — but open to students of any major, not only Honors College students (hotel and restaurant management is a confirmed eligible major). Students carry out one part-time research project for one term, under the direct guidance of one faculty mentor.

Scale of competition: roughly ~20 students university-wide receive this scholarship each term across all majors combined — a concrete sign that this is not an "apply and get in" program.

ENTRY REQUIREMENTS

StandingSophomore, Junior, or Senior
GPAMinimum 3.0/4.0

APPLICATION MATERIALS & PROCESS

  • A faculty member must agree to mentor first — this is a prerequisite; there is no applying without a confirmed mentor.
  • A 500-word research proposal — written in the student's own words (not written by the faculty member), developed in consultation with the mentor; it must state a realistic goal clearly, describe the mentor's role and connect it to the mentor's existing research direction, and contain no spelling/grammar errors.
  • A 1–2 page letter of recommendation from the faculty mentor — sent directly by the faculty member to the PURS Program Manager by email, not submitted by the student.
  • If the research involves human subjects (surveys/interviews) → the student must consult the mentor about the IRB approval process before beginning (see Compliance below).

COMMITMENTS DURING THE TERM

  • A minimum of 6–7 hours/week, totaling 100 hours over the term, of research under faculty supervision.
  • Attend a PURS orientation during the first week of the term.
  • Attend at least one research webinar/event during the term.
  • Complete pre- and post-program surveys.
  • Presenting a poster at Undergraduate Research Day is only "highly encouraged," NOT required — students still fulfill all program requirements without presenting a poster, though doing so is a strong opportunity for presentation practice and networking.

COMPLIANCE REQUIREMENTS — CITI TRAINING + IRB

  • CITI Responsible Conduct of Research (RCR) training is required for every student researcher at UH, even under direct faculty supervision — applies to SURF, PURS, and Senior Honors Thesis programs since Spring 2020. Registered through the CITI Program site, selecting "University of Houston" as the institution.
  • Certificate submission deadline: the Official Reporting Day (ORD) — the 12th class day of a Fall/Spring term, or the 4th class day of a Summer term.
  • IRB (Institutional Review Board) approval is required if the research observes, interviews, or surveys human subjects — very likely applicable to behavioral/cognitive hospitality research. Standard review takes 2–3 weeks once the application clears department/college-level routing, but it's recommended to submit at least one month before the planned data-collection start date to allow buffer time.
  • All research participants — including the student — must complete CITI training BEFORE the IRB application can be approved; the two requirements are sequential, not parallel.

Planning consequence: if PURS Spring 2027 begins ~January 2027 and IRB approval is needed before data collection, CITI training and the IRB submission should be completed starting at the end of Fall 2026 — not delayed until PURS officially begins — to avoid losing the first few weeks of the term to administrative approval.

WHAT 6–7 HOURS/WEEK ACTUALLY LOOKS LIKE

There is no fixed template — the exact responsibilities depend entirely on the nature of the project and the mentor's assignments. The most common tasks, ranked by how frequently they come up:

Type of workWhat it actually looks like
Literature reviewFinding, reading, and summarizing articles/studies related to the mentor's topic — the most common task, present in nearly every project
Data entry & managementEntering survey/experiment data into software (SPSS, Excel), cleaning data, organizing databases
Data collectionRunning surveys, interviewing participants, recruiting participants, sometimes going to a field site to collect real-world data
Preliminary analysisRunning descriptive statistics, helping code variables — not deep analysis (that remains the mentor's or a PhD student's work)
Preparing research materialsPreparing questionnaires, interview protocols, and IRB (ethics application) materials if the project involves human subjects

Applied to survey-based hospitality behavior research more broadly: this kind of research tends to lean on quantitative surveys and behavioral analysis, so the most likely real tasks are reading hospitality-industry literature, helping design and distribute surveys, entering and cleaning survey data, and summarizing literature — not physical lab work.

WHAT THE STUDENT ACTUALLY CONTRIBUTES — NOT JUST "ODD JOBS"

  • Frees up time for work that's necessary but time-consuming: literature review and data entry take the most hours but require the least specialized expertise — the mentor can spend the freed-up time on deeper analysis and writing instead of doing everything alone.
  • A fresh perspective and new energy: research notes that new students often bring "fresh minds" that help faculty see a problem from a different angle.
  • Future co-authorship potential: on some projects, if a student's contribution is substantial enough, they may be listed as a co-author on publication — not automatic, and dependent on the mentor and the level of contribution.
  • "Broader impact" for grant applications: for NSF grants specifically, undergraduate involvement boosts the "broader impact" score when faculty write funding proposals — meaning faculty have a real, practical incentive, not just goodwill, to take on a student.

WHAT THE MENTOR GETS IN RETURN — NOT JUST "CHARITY"

Taking on a student isn't one-sided sacrifice by the faculty member — there are real benefits, even if not everyone says so out loud:

  • Higher overall research output — cited research shows faculty who mentor students tend to have a more productive research agenda, not slowed down by the time spent teaching a student.
  • Improved teaching quality — mentoring students leads to improved classroom engagement and more accessible teaching styles, since one-on-one mentoring helps faculty better understand how students learn.
  • Interpersonal / socio-emotional benefits — faculty genuinely enjoy watching a student grow and building a long-term professional relationship; a real motivator, not just marketing language.
  • "Academic DNA transfer" — passing on research thinking and methods to the next generation; a faculty member whose student goes on to a PhD or becomes a researcher gains a form of academic legacy.
  • Indirect credit toward grant applications (as noted above).

Worth knowing, so as not to over-romanticize it: one source on mentoring undergraduates states plainly that "the academy itself is a system that provides few incentives to mentor... These relationships are not valued when it comes time for promotion or tenure. Grants are not awarded based on mentoring productivity." In other words, a faculty member's motivation to take on a student mostly comes from personal passion for teaching and watching students grow — not from formal institutional reward. This is exactly why choosing a mentor who is genuinely inclined to mentor matters, and it explains why some otherwise-eligible faculty still decline to take on more students — not for lack of capacity, but because mentoring isn't a mandatory obligation.

PUBLICATION POTENTIAL

UH maintains an official page, "Publications with Undergraduate Co-Authors," listing real published papers with undergraduate co-authors — for example, on guest support for smoke-free policies at a homeless shelter, and a qualitative study on a tobacco-cessation training program, among others across several journals.

However, the correct way to read this: it's a multi-year aggregate across several programs (SURF, REACH, independent projects — not PURS alone), not evidence that "doing PURS guarantees a publication." Publication depends on (1) whether the mentor's project is publication-bound, (2) whether the student's contribution is substantial enough to merit co-authorship, and (3) time — most publications take longer than one term to materialize, so PURS (~15 weeks) is often the starting point of a longer project, with any resulting paper appearing later, possibly while already in the MS or PhD.

Bottom line: publication potential is not guaranteed — it depends entirely on the chosen faculty mentor and whether the project continues beyond the PURS term.

DEFINITION & HISTORY OF ETA SIGMA DELTA (ESD)

ESD (Eta Sigma Delta) is the International Hospitality Management Honor Society, founded in 1978 by a group of students at the Whittemore School of Business and Economics, University of New Hampshire (now the Peter T. Paul College of Business and Economics) — the founding rationale was that other fields already had their own honor societies, but hospitality/tourism, one of the largest industries in the world, did not yet have one. Today it has more than 90 chapters worldwide, operating under the sponsorship of International CHRIE.

Five "Points of Honor" ESD upholds (symbolic, read at induction ceremonies): Creativity, Ethics, Excellence, Leadership, Service.

PROPOSED 12-CREDIT PRIORITY FOR THE 4+1 STAGE

4+1 allows up to 12 graduate credit hours before BS graduation. Based on the Research Track structure above, here is a proposed priority order to both target the PhD goal and use the full 12-hour allowance:

PriorityCourseCreditReason
1HRMA 6330 — Statistical Data Analysis3Foundation directly feeding into HRMA 8303 (Multivariate Analysis) at the PhD level
2HRMA 6382 — Methods of Research3Foundation directly feeding into HRMA 8304 (Qualitative Design) at the PhD level
3HRMA 6190 — Research Proposal1Start practicing proposal writing as early as possible — direct groundwork for the dissertation proposal
4HRMA 6101 — Colloquium1A 1-credit course, easy to fit into a BS schedule, builds an early seminar-attendance habit
5HRMA 7369 — Financial Assets Planning & Management3The remaining Research Track core course — best taken early to lighten the final MS year
6HRMA 7353 — Services Management1 (of the remaining 12h)Lower priority — can be swapped for a different Support/Focus course if scheduling doesn't align

Priorities 1–3 (7 credit hours) are the "must-take" group — the three methodology courses that bridge directly into the PhD. Priorities 4–6 are "nice to take if the schedule allows" — they ease the final MS year but don't directly affect the PhD foundation.
